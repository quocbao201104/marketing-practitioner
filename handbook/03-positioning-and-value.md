# 03 — Positioning and Value

## 1. Positioning as a comparative mental model

Positioning concerns how an offering is understood relative to alternatives in the customer's mind. It is not synonymous with a tagline, slogan, or statement of aspiration. Research on categorization and positioning shows that category membership and perceived discrepancy can shape how consumers interpret brands and alternatives [R05]. Other empirical work challenges the assumption that perceived differentiation is always the dominant source of brand choice and emphasizes the importance of distinctiveness and recognizability [R06].

A practical positioning model therefore treats two questions separately:

- **Differentiation:** why should this option be preferred to relevant alternatives?
- **Distinctiveness:** can the market recognize and remember this option reliably?

They are complementary rather than interchangeable.

## 2. Category as a mental shelf

A category reduces interpretation cost by giving the customer a familiar frame. It also imposes expectations. Once an offering is understood as a CRM, analytics platform, marketplace, consultancy, or newsletter, the customer imports assumptions about table stakes, competitors, pricing, risk, and evaluation criteria.

Category choice therefore creates a trade-off. A familiar category lowers education cost but can intensify comparison. A novel category can highlight a new mechanism or problem frame but requires the organization to teach the market how to evaluate it.

When category is uncertain, a useful diagnostic question is: **What will the customer initially mistake this for?** The answer often reveals the actual mental shelf against which communication must work.

## 3. Positioning inputs

A complete positioning analysis should consider:

- target context;
- category or mental shelf;
- current alternatives;
- customer job or desired progress;
- principal buying criteria;
- table stakes;
- primary value;
- differentiating mechanism or capability;
- distinctive cues;
- reason to believe;
- strategic trade-offs;
- conditions under which the offer is not appropriate.

The analysis should be grounded in both customer evidence and actual product capability. Customer desire can establish relevance; it cannot create a product fact that does not exist.

## 4. Value proposition as a structured relationship

A value proposition is not a list of benefits. It links a particular customer in a particular situation to a meaningful improvement relative to an alternative, supported by a credible reason to believe.

A useful conceptual chain is:

```text
WHO
→ SITUATION / JOB
→ CURRENT ALTERNATIVE
→ DESIRED PROGRESS
→ PRIMARY VALUE
→ REASON TO BELIEVE
→ TRADE-OFF
```

This structure prevents three frequent errors: describing features without their decision value, describing benefits without a target context, and asserting value without comparative reference.

## 5. From feature to decision value

Features become strategically meaningful only through consequences. The interpretation can move through several levels:

```text
CAPABILITY
→ FUNCTIONAL OUTCOME
→ DECISION OR BUSINESS VALUE
→ RISK / EMOTIONAL VALUE
```

The final level requires particular caution. Emotional and risk-related benefits are persuasive precisely because they are consequential; they should not be inferred merely because they sound plausible. Customer research should support them.

## 6. Proof and value are inseparable

A promise has little strategic value if the market cannot believe it. Proof can include mechanisms, demonstrations, data, credible customer evidence, guarantees, product behavior, third-party validation, or constraints that make the claim testable.

The principle "proof before adjectives" follows from this relationship. Promotional descriptors such as *innovative*, *trusted*, *powerful*, or *best* are weak substitutes for evidence. Research on linguistic concreteness also suggests that concrete language can affect perceived listening and customer responses in the contexts studied, while not supporting a universal rule that concreteness always wins [R07]. Processing fluency research similarly supports attention to ease of processing without turning clarity into a deterministic persuasion formula [R08].

## 7. Positioning requires trade-offs

Credible positioning usually contains exclusion. A product cannot simultaneously occupy every desirable extreme: lowest cost, highest power, simplest use, maximum customization, zero setup, and enterprise depth. Attempting to claim all dimensions reduces informational value because nothing is prioritized.

Trade-offs can be explicit or structural. A high-control system may require more setup; a highly standardized product may reject edge cases; a premium service may deliberately limit volume. These boundaries can strengthen positioning when they are connected to the customer's actual priorities.

## 8. Competitor whitespace is not customer value

Competitive analysis often identifies messages that competitors do not use. This can reveal an opportunity, but absence is not evidence of relevance. A message can be unique and still unimportant.

Whitespace should therefore be tested against:

- customer buying criteria;
- unresolved needs;
- category expectations;
- alternative behavior;
- credible product advantage.

A strategically useful distinction must matter to the customer and be supportable by the product.

## 9. Positioning is revisable

Positioning is a current strategic model, not an eternal brand essence. It should be reviewed when customer composition, product capability, competitive structure, distribution, regulation, or the dominant alternative changes.

The review should not be triggered by novelty alone. Consistency has value. Change is warranted when the existing model no longer explains how priority customers evaluate the offer or when a better-supported model materially improves decision quality.

## 10. Positioning output

A compact positioning record should state:

```text
TARGET CONTEXT
CATEGORY / MENTAL SHELF
CURRENT ALTERNATIVE
JOB / DESIRED PROGRESS
PRIMARY VALUE
TABLE STAKES
DIFFERENTIATOR
DISTINCTIVE CUES
REASON TO BELIEVE
TRADE-OFF
CLAIMS NOT SUPPORTED
MATERIAL UNCERTAINTIES
```

This record is the strategic input to message design. Copy begins only after these choices are sufficiently resolved for the task.
