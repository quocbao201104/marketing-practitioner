# Commercial Design Evidence Ledger

This supplementary ledger defines the `CDxx` source identifiers used by `../handbook/10-commercial-design-pricing-and-terms.md`.

The chapter is a practitioner synthesis of established pricing, product-line, business-model, platform, organizational, and customer-management research. No single source establishes the chapter's four-part decomposition.

For each source, preserve what it establishes and what it does not. Do not convert a source-specific result into a universal pricing rule.

---

## Conceptual and review parents

### [CD01] Kienzler & Kowalkowski — pricing-strategy research review

Mario Kienzler & Christian Kowalkowski (2017). **Pricing strategy: A review of 22 years of marketing research.** *Journal of Business Research*, 78, 101–110.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2017.05.005

Evidence type: peer-reviewed literature review / content analysis of 515 articles published between 1995 and 2016.

Use: supports treating pricing strategy as a broad mature research area rather than a scalar price-point tactic; provides prior-art pressure against novelty claims.

Boundary: does not establish the project's four-part synthesis, one universally optimal pricing strategy, or transfer of reviewed findings without context.

### [CD02] Nagle, Müller & Gruyaert — strategic-pricing practitioner parent

Thomas T. Nagle, Georg Müller & Evert Gruyaert (2023). **The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing: A Guide to Growing More Profitably.** 7th ed. Routledge.

ISBN: 9781032016825 / 9781000915969.

Evidence type: established strategic-pricing text / practitioner conceptual parent, not an empirical study.

Use: parent for offer configurations, bundles, price metrics, price fences, pricing policy, negotiation, promotions, competition, price setting, price sensitivity, and financial analysis.

Boundary: does not provide empirical effect sizes, a universal optimal rule, or the exact project synthesis.

### [CD03] Moorthy — self-selection and product-line design

K. Sridhar Moorthy (1984). **Market Segmentation, Self-Selection, and Product Line Design.** *Marketing Science*, 3(4), 288–307.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.3.4.288

Evidence type: formal economic/marketing theory.

Use: distinguishes direct segment addressability from consumer self-selection; supports coupled product/configuration and price reasoning and the selection/allocation distinction.

Boundary: does not establish a universal tier count, universal use of self-selection, or empirical effect sizes for current SaaS/retail settings.

### [CD04] Armstrong — nonlinear pricing review

Mark Armstrong (2016). **Nonlinear Pricing.** *Annual Review of Economics*, 8, 583–614.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080614-115650

Evidence type: scholarly review/theory synthesis.

Use: parent for nonlinear tariffs, quantity/bundle discounts, product-line pricing, price discrimination, upgrades, add-ons, and screening; supports scalar price != pricing architecture.

Boundary: does not establish that nonlinear pricing is preferable in every setting or one universal tariff design.

## Commercial evidence and decision boundaries

### [CD05] Schmidt & Bijmolt — hypothetical WTP meta-analysis

Jonas Schmidt & Tammo H. A. Bijmolt (2020). **Accurately measuring willingness to pay for consumer goods: a meta-analysis of the hypothetical bias.** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*, 48, 499–518.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-019-00666-6

Evidence type: meta-analysis of 77 studies from 47 papers and 115 effect sizes after exclusions.

Use: supports `stated/hypothetical WTP != revealed/real WTP`, material hypothetical bias, and rejection of the folklore that indirect WTP methods are automatically more accurate than direct methods.

Boundary: the reported average bias is not a correction factor for a new study; direct methods are not universally superior; hypothetical research is not automatically useless.

### [CD06] Tian & Feinberg — subscription price menus

Longxiu Tian & Fred M. Feinberg (2020). **Optimizing Price Menus for Duration Discounts: A Subscription Selectivity Field Experiment.** *Marketing Science*, 39(6), 1181–1198.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2020.1265

Evidence type: randomized online pricing experiment plus statistical choice model.

Use: supports jointly considering overall subscription opt-in and conditional plan choice and separating duration/commitment structure from one scalar conversion outcome.

Boundary: does not establish a universal duration discount or transfer the studied menu optimum to other products.

### [CD07] Lewis, Singh & Fay — nonlinear shipping fees

Michael Lewis, Vishal Singh & Scott Fay (2006). **An Empirical Study of the Impact of Nonlinear Shipping and Handling Fees on Purchase Incidence and Expenditure Decisions.** *Marketing Science*, 25(1), 51–64.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1050.0150

Evidence type: empirical analysis using an online retailer's varied shipping-fee schedules.

Use: demonstrates that shipping schedules can affect purchase incidence and basket size and provides a counterexample where additional sales do not imply profit after foregone shipping revenue and heterogeneous response.

Boundary: does not establish that free shipping is generally unprofitable or one universal threshold.

### [CD08] Yoganarasimhan, Barzegary & Pani — free-trial duration

Hema Yoganarasimhan, Ebrahim Barzegary & Abhishek Pani (2023). **Design and Evaluation of Optimal Free Trials.** *Management Science*, 69(6), 3220–3240.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4507

Evidence type: large-scale field experiment plus model-based evaluation.

Use: supports treating trials as access/learning commercial interventions and shows trial duration can affect acquisition, retention, and profitability in the studied SaaS setting.

Boundary: does not establish that shorter trials are always better or one universal trial duration.

### [CD09] Santana, Dallas & Morwitz — drip pricing

Shelle Santana, Steven K. Dallas & Vicki G. Morwitz (2020). **Consumer Reactions to Drip Pricing.** *Marketing Science*, 39(1), 188–210.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2019.1207

Evidence type: six consumer studies.

Use: supports separating underlying commercial terms from presentation/reveal timing and shows later surcharge disclosure can affect initial choice and satisfaction in studied settings.

Boundary: does not establish one universal fee effect or justify hiding material terms.

### [CD10] Blake, Moshary, Sweeney & Tadelis — price salience field experiment

Tom Blake, Sarah Moshary, Kane Sweeney & Steve Tadelis (2021). **Price Salience and Product Choice.** *Marketing Science*, 40(4), 619–636.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2020.1261

Evidence type: large-scale randomized field experiment on StubHub.

Use: supports `commercial terms != representation of commercial terms` and demonstrates setting-specific effects of upfront versus later fee disclosure on comparison and purchase behavior.

Boundary: does not establish a universal presentation tactic or that lower salience is desirable.

### [CD11] Anderson & Simester — long-run promotion-depth effects

Eric T. Anderson & Duncan I. Simester (2004). **Long-Run Effects of Promotion Depth on New Versus Established Customers: Three Field Studies.** *Marketing Science*, 23(1), 4–20.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1030.0040

Evidence type: three large-scale field experiments.

Use: supports `short-run response != long-run effect` and preserving customer history/cohort when promotion interpretation depends on it.

Boundary: does not establish a universal promotion-depth policy or transfer measured effects beyond the studied context.

### [CD12] Dubé & Misra — personalized pricing and consumer welfare

Jean-Pierre Dubé & Sanjog Misra (2023). **Personalized Pricing and Consumer Welfare.** *Journal of Political Economy*, 131(1), 131–189.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1086/720793

Evidence type: randomized controlled pricing field experiment, personalized-price construction, and field validation.

Use: demonstrates that firm profit, total consumer surplus, and distributional outcomes can diverge; supports preserving the selection/allocation regime rather than only the resulting price.

Boundary: does not establish that personalized pricing always harms consumers, does not measure perceived fairness or trust, and does not supply a universal normative rule for differential pricing.

## Organizational, business-model, platform, and lifecycle boundaries

### [CD13] Homburg, Jensen & Hahn — pricing authority

Christian Homburg, Ove Jensen & Alexander Hahn (2012). **How to Organize Pricing? Vertical Delegation and Horizontal Dispersion of Pricing Authority.** *Journal of Marketing*, 76(5), 49–69.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.11.0251

Evidence type: empirical organizational-design research.

Use: supports rejecting a universal Marketing owner for pricing and treating authority/governance as context rather than a Commercial Design primitive.

Boundary: does not establish one universal organizational design or causal prescription for every firm.

### [CD14] Teece — business-model boundary

David J. Teece (2010). **Business Models, Business Strategy and Innovation.** *Long Range Planning*, 43(2–3), 172–194.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2009.07.003

Evidence type: conceptual strategy/business-model article.

Use: broad parent for value creation, delivery, and capture; supports keeping Commercial Design inside a larger business-model envelope.

Boundary: does not establish the four-part Commercial Design model or a pricing optimization procedure.

### [CD15] Rochet & Tirole — two-sided platform pricing

Jean-Charles Rochet & Jean Tirole (2003). **Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets.** *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 1(4), 990–1029.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1162/154247603322493212

Evidence type: formal economic theory.

Use: supports explicit actor-side/payment-flow reasoning when participation on one side affects value on another and `price on one side != total monetization architecture`.

Boundary: does not establish that every marketplace matches the modeled setting, a universal take rate/subsidy, or that Commercial Design should become full platform strategy.

### [CD16] Ascarza — retention targeting and treatment response

Eva Ascarza (2018). **Retention Futility: Targeting High-Risk Customers Might Be Ineffective.** *Journal of Marketing Research*, 55(1), 80–98.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.16.0163

Evidence type: two field experiments combined with predictive modeling.

Use: supports `predicted churn risk != responsiveness to a retention intervention` and the distinction between customer state/risk prediction and causal treatment effect.

Boundary: does not establish that high-risk customers should never be targeted, a universal retention policy, or that churn prediction lacks value.

# Cross-source synthesis boundaries

The following four-part decomposition is a project synthesis, not a claim attributed to any one source:

```text
CONFIGURATION / ENTITLEMENT
PAYMENT / VALUE-CAPTURE ARCHITECTURE
RELATIONSHIP / RISK TERMS
SELECTION / ALLOCATION RULE
```

Likewise:

```text
COMMERCIAL DESIGN
!= COMMERCIAL GOVERNANCE
!= COMMERCIAL INSTANCE
```

is a practitioner boundary synthesized across pricing-organization, negotiation, and state-representation concerns. CD13 supports the importance of pricing authority but does not itself propose this exact wording.

The following are also project-level inferential guardrails rather than quoted laws:

```text
MARKET-DESIRABLE
!= ECONOMICALLY ATTRACTIVE
!= OPERATIONALLY FEASIBLE
!= PERMISSIBLE
!= AUTHORIZED

PARAMETER UNCERTAINTY
!= DECISION UNCERTAINTY
```

Use them to prevent category errors, not as empirical claims with universal effect sizes.

# Known evidence boundaries

This ledger is not a complete pricing bibliography. The runtime chapter deliberately avoids universal prescriptions in areas where this initial evidence set is not sufficient, including:

- B2B negotiated pricing and contract/risk-allocation effect sizes;
- universal return/refund or guarantee policies;
- universal grandfathering/migration rules;
- one optimal price-experiment or value-of-information procedure;
- universal fairness/privacy conclusions for personalized pricing;
- universal usage-based or outcome-based B2B price-metric selection;
- one optimal marketplace commission or channel-contract structure.

When a task depends on those questions, retain uncertainty, use authoritative current domain evidence where available, and avoid upgrading adjacent evidence into a general rule.
