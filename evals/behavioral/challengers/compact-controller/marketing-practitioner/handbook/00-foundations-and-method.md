# 00 — Foundations and Method

## 1. Marketing as a market-learning discipline

A useful definition of marketing must extend beyond promotion, content production, media buying, or conversion tactics. Market-orientation research describes organizations as generating market intelligence, disseminating it, and responding to it across functions [R01]. Related work emphasizes customer orientation, competitor orientation, and coordinated response [R02]. These traditions support an operating view in which marketing is partly a **learning system**: the organization observes a market, constructs a provisional model of customer and competitive reality, makes choices, acts, observes response, and revises its model.

This repository therefore uses the following analytical sequence:

```text
OBSERVE
→ DIAGNOSE
→ RESEARCH
→ HYPOTHESIZE
→ CHOOSE
→ COMMUNICATE / ACT
→ MEASURE
→ LEARN
→ REUSE
```

The sequence is deliberately broader than a campaign workflow. Its endpoint is not publication. The endpoint is a better-grounded future decision.

## 2. Three levels that must not be collapsed

Marketing work becomes unreliable when evidence, interpretation, and action are merged into a single narrative. The handbook separates three levels.

### 2.1 Empirical level

The empirical level concerns what was observed or measured. It includes interview statements, behavior, transaction records, traffic, conversion events, customer-service interactions, experiments, and other source material.

### 2.2 Explanatory level

The explanatory level concerns what the evidence may mean: mechanisms, patterns, causal hypotheses, segment differences, perceived alternatives, barriers, or decision criteria. Explanations remain provisional unless the design supports stronger inference.

### 2.3 Decision level

The decision level concerns what the organization should do under its goals, constraints, opportunity costs, and risk tolerance. Evidence informs a decision but does not mechanically determine it. Two firms can rationally make different decisions from similar evidence because their economics, capabilities, strategic priorities, or tolerance for error differ.

This distinction prevents a common category error: treating a customer statement as a strategy, a dashboard movement as a cause, or a statistical result as a complete business decision.

## 3. Types of marketing questions

Method selection should follow the question rather than the tool already available.

| Question type | Primary concern | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | What is happening? | measurement, logs, surveys, observation |
| Interpretive | How is the situation experienced or understood? | interviews, qualitative observation, open text |
| Associational | What varies with what? | observational quantitative data |
| Predictive | What is likely to happen next? | validated predictive models, historical patterns |
| Causal | What would change under an intervention? | randomized experiments or defensible causal designs |
| Strategic | What should we choose? | evidence plus objectives, economics, constraints, risk |

The categories overlap in practical projects but should remain conceptually distinct. In particular, predictive usefulness does not establish causal leverage, and descriptive recurrence in qualitative research does not establish market prevalence.

## 4. Evidence is multidimensional

A single confidence score usually hides more than it clarifies. Evidence strength varies along several dimensions:

- **source quality** — how directly and reliably the source represents the phenomenon;
- **sample adequacy** — whether the sample supports the type of claim being made;
- **scope match** — whether the evidence concerns the segment, market, product state, and period of interest;
- **design strength** — whether the method supports description, association, prediction, or causation;
- **freshness** — whether the market or product may have materially changed;
- **consistency** — whether independent evidence converges or conflicts;
- **independence** — whether apparently separate observations derive from genuinely separate sources.

This multidimensional view is intentionally conservative. It makes uncertainty visible rather than laundering it into pseudo-precision.

## 5. Decision quality, not activity volume

Marketing teams can produce high volumes of content, experiments, and reports while learning very little. Activity is not a reliable proxy for value. Decision quality improves when work clarifies at least one of the following:

- which customers or contexts deserve priority;
- which alternatives customers actually consider;
- which value proposition is credible and relevant;
- which claims can be supported;
- which mechanism plausibly explains a change;
- which intervention should be tested;
- which prior belief should be retained, weakened, or abandoned.

The model therefore allows **inconclusive** and **do nothing** as legitimate outcomes. When evidence is weak, additional action can destroy interpretability by changing several variables at once.

## 6. The status of frameworks and heuristics

Marketing practice contains many useful frameworks that are not scientific laws. A framework should be evaluated by whether it improves attention, comparison, decision consistency, or communication—not by whether it appears mathematically precise.

Accordingly, this handbook uses three epistemic labels implicitly:

- **established concept** — supported by a mature research or methodological tradition;
- **practical heuristic** — a useful decision aid whose form is not itself a validated law;
- **working hypothesis** — a context-dependent proposition that requires evidence in the target setting.

Readers and agents should preserve these distinctions. A heuristic such as evaluating an ICP by pain, product fit, economics, reachability, and strategic fit is a structured checklist; multiplying those factors does not create a validated market equation.

## 7. Marketing as constrained inference

The practitioner rarely has complete information. Marketing therefore operates under uncertainty and must continually balance the cost of information against the cost of being wrong.

A useful rule is to raise the evidence threshold when a decision is expensive, irreversible, reputationally sensitive, legally material, or difficult to reverse. Conversely, a bounded and reversible intervention can be reasonable under greater uncertainty if downside is controlled and learning value is high.

This principle connects research, experimentation, messaging, and ethics. It explains why an uncertain headline hypothesis may justify a small test while an uncertain medical claim, security promise, or major pricing change requires a much stronger basis.

## 8. Core methodological commitments

The remainder of the handbook follows eight commitments:

1. research is tied to a decision;
2. source material is kept distinct from interpretation;
3. counterevidence is actively retained;
4. scope is explicit;
5. positioning precedes message expression;
6. proof constrains copy;
7. measurement is separated from causal storytelling;
8. learning is preserved with boundary conditions and failure states.

Together these commitments define the intellectual center of Marketing Practitioner: **marketing is the disciplined conversion of market evidence into bounded decisions and revisable learning**.
