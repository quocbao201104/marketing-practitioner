# Selected Bibliography

This bibliography prioritizes peer-reviewed research, academic monographs, methodological texts, and authoritative research guidance. It is selective rather than exhaustive. Practitioner sources are identified separately and are not treated as scientific laws.

The handbook cites references by identifier, e.g. `[R01]`.

---

## Market orientation and organizational learning

### [R01] Kohli & Jaworski — Market Orientation

Kohli, A. K., & Jaworski, B. J. (1990). **Market Orientation: The Construct, Research Propositions, and Managerial Implications.** *Journal of Marketing, 54*(2), 1–18. DOI: 10.1177/002224299005400201.

Use: market-intelligence generation, dissemination, and organizational responsiveness.

### [R02] Narver & Slater — Market Orientation and Profitability

Narver, J. C., & Slater, S. F. (1990). **The Effect of a Market Orientation on Business Profitability.** *Journal of Marketing, 54*(4), 20–35. DOI: 10.1177/002224299005400403.

Use: customer orientation, competitor orientation, and interfunctional coordination. The empirical relationship in the study should not be represented as a universal deterministic law.

### [R09] Walsh & Ungson — Organizational Memory

Walsh, J. P., & Ungson, G. R. (1991). **Organizational Memory.** *Academy of Management Review, 16*(1), 57–91. DOI: 10.5465/AMR.1991.4278992.

Use: acquisition, retention, and retrieval of organizational information; conceptual basis for distinguishing useful memory from file accumulation.

## Segmentation, jobs, and positioning

### [R03] Wedel & Kamakura — Market Segmentation

Wedel, M., & Kamakura, W. A. (2000). **Market Segmentation: Conceptual and Methodological Foundations** (2nd ed.). Springer / Kluwer Academic Publishers. DOI: 10.1007/978-1-4615-4651-1.

Use: customer heterogeneity, segmentation bases, profiling, and methodological foundations.

### [R04] Christensen — Jobs-to-be-Done

Christensen, C. M. (2016). **The “Jobs to be Done” Theory of Innovation.** Harvard Business Review IdeaCast; associated with *Competing Against Luck*.

Use: practitioner lens for customer progress and situational alternatives. Treated as a useful theory/practice lens rather than a universal replacement for segmentation.

### [R05] Sujan & Bettman — Brand and Category Positioning

Sujan, M., & Bettman, J. R. (1989). **The Effects of Brand Positioning Strategies on Consumers’ Brand and Category Perceptions: Some Insights from Schema Research.** *Journal of Marketing Research, 26*(4). DOI: 10.1177/002224378902600407.

Use: category perception, discrepancy, and positioning effects.

### [R06] Romaniuk, Sharp & Ehrenberg — Differentiation and Distinctiveness

Romaniuk, J., Sharp, B., & Ehrenberg, A. (2007). **Evidence concerning the Importance of Perceived Brand Differentiation.** *Australasian Marketing Journal, 15*(2), 42–54. DOI: 10.1016/S1441-3582(07)70042-3.

Use: empirical challenge to simplistic differentiation doctrine and support for treating distinctiveness as a separate question.

## Language, communication, and copy

### [R07] Packard & Berger — Concrete Language

Packard, G., & Berger, J. (2021). **How Concrete Language Shapes Customer Satisfaction.** *Journal of Consumer Research, 47*(5), 787–806. DOI: 10.1093/jcr/ucaa038.

Use: evidence on linguistic concreteness, perceived listening, and downstream customer responses in the contexts studied. Not a universal rule that concrete wording always dominates.

### [R08] Kostyk, Leonhardt & Niculescu — Processing Fluency

Kostyk, A., Leonhardt, J. M., & Niculescu, M. (2021). **Processing fluency scale development for consumer research.** *International Journal of Market Research, 63*(3). DOI: 10.1177/1470785319877137.

Use: processing fluency as a consumer-research construct; supports attention to ease of processing without converting clarity into a deterministic persuasion formula.

## Customer and user research

### [R10] UK Government Digital Service — In-depth Interviews

UK Government Digital Service. **Using in-depth interviews.** GOV.UK Service Manual.

Use: interviews for understanding context, experience, problems, and language; not population prevalence or causal effect size.

### [R21] UK Government Digital Service — Research Planning and Analysis

UK Government Digital Service. **Plan user research for your service; Analyse a research session; Learning about users and their needs.** GOV.UK Service Manual.

Use: research questions, iterative research, analysis of source material, and treatment of unsupported stakeholder opinion as assumption rather than evidence.

## Causal inference and experimentation

### [R11] Hernán & Robins — Causal Inference

Hernán, M. A., & Robins, J. M. (2020). **Causal Inference: What If.** Chapman & Hall/CRC; freely available from the authors.

Use: counterfactual thinking, distinction between descriptive/predictive and causal questions, identification assumptions.

### [R12] Kohavi et al. — Controlled Experiments on the Web

Kohavi, R., Longbotham, R., Sommerfield, D., & Henne, R. M. (2009). **Controlled experiments on the web: survey and practical guide.** *Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, 18*, 140–181. DOI: 10.1007/s10618-008-0114-1.

Use: randomized online experiments, power, metrics, randomization, implementation pitfalls, and experimentation practice.

## International marketing and culture

### [R13] Theodosiou & Leonidou — Standardization vs Adaptation

Theodosiou, M., & Leonidou, L. C. (2003). **Standardization versus adaptation of international marketing strategy: an integrative assessment of the empirical research.** *International Business Review, 12*(2), 141–171. DOI: 10.1016/S0969-5931(02)00094-X.

Use: empirical review showing that standardization/adaptation decisions depend on foreign-market circumstances and marketing-mix layer.

### [R14] de Mooij & Hofstede — Cross-Cultural Consumer Behavior

de Mooij, M., & Hofstede, G. (2011). **Cross-Cultural Consumer Behavior: A Review of Research Findings.** *Journal of International Consumer Marketing, 23*(3–4), 181–192. DOI: 10.1080/08961530.2011.578057.

Use: cultural variation as a possible influence on cognition, emotion, communication, and branding. Not a deterministic individual classifier.

### [R15] Steenkamp & Baumgartner — Measurement Invariance

Steenkamp, J.-B. E. M., & Baumgartner, H. (1998). **Assessing Measurement Invariance in Cross-National Consumer Research.** *Journal of Consumer Research, 25*(1), 78–90. DOI: 10.1086/209528.

Use: requirement for measurement equivalence before strong cross-national comparison of constructs.

### [R16] Hornikx, Janssen & O’Keefe — Cultural Adaptation Meta-analysis

Hornikx, J., Janssen, A., & O’Keefe, D. J. (2026). **Cultural Value Adaptation in Advertising is Effective, But Not Dependable: A Meta-Analysis of 25 Years of Experimental Research.** *International Journal of Business Communication, 63*(2), 407–431. DOI: 10.1177/23294884231199088. First published online in 2023.

Use: average effects of cultural-value adaptation accompanied by substantial uncertainty; supports using cultural models as hypotheses rather than campaign rules.

### [R17] Steel & Taras — Within-Country Cultural Variation

Steel, P., & Taras, V. (2010). **Culture as a consequence: A multi-level multivariate meta-analysis of the effects of individual and country characteristics on work-related cultural values.** *Journal of International Management, 16*(3), 211–233. DOI: 10.1016/j.intman.2010.06.002.

Use: substantial within-country variation and limitations of applying national averages to individuals.

### [R18] Aaker & Williams — Emotional Appeals Across Cultures

Aaker, J. L., & Williams, P. (1998). **Empathy versus Pride: The Influence of Emotional Appeals across Cultures.** *Journal of Consumer Research, 25*(3), 241–261. DOI: 10.1086/209537.

Use: experimental evidence that cultural/self-related orientation can interact with emotional persuasion.

### [R19] Aaker & Lee — Self-Construal and Regulatory Focus

Aaker, J. L., & Lee, A. Y. (2001). **“I” Seek Pleasures and “We” Avoid Pains: The Role of Self-Regulatory Goals in Information Processing and Persuasion.** *Journal of Consumer Research, 28*(1), 33–49. DOI: 10.1086/321946.

Use: interaction of self-view, regulatory orientation, information processing, and persuasion; suitable for hypothesis generation rather than national-level personalization rules.

### [R20] Barnes & Shavitt — Consensus Cues

Barnes, A. J., & Shavitt, S. (2024). **Top Rated or Best Seller? Cultural Differences in Responses to Attitudinal versus Behavioral Consensus Cues.** *Journal of Consumer Research, 51*(2), 276–297. DOI: 10.1093/jcr/ucad074.

Use: evidence that social proof is not a single universal cue and that different consensus signals can carry different diagnostic value by context.

## Evidence policy for citations

A citation in this repository supports only the proposition described by the cited work and the boundary stated in the handbook. It should not be interpreted as evidence that:

- every effect replicates in every market;
- a theory predicts every individual;
- a practical framework is scientifically validated in its exact operational form;
- an observed association implies a causal intervention effect;
- one source is sufficient to settle a contested marketing question.

Where possible, future editions should add systematic reviews, replications, and competing findings rather than expanding the bibliography only with sources that support the current model.
