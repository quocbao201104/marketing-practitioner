# Report and Artifact Planning and Presentation

Use for marketing reports, research syntheses, decision briefs, guides, reusable references, workbooks/action guides, and other document-like marketing artifacts when their information architecture, representation, retrieval, or working-use structure needs deliberate design. This is an operating contract, not evidence that one layout improves business outcomes. It applies across Markdown, HTML, PDF, slides, printable documents, and similar delivery forms; adapt to the requested medium without changing the evidence or adopted decisions.

Do not activate this reference merely because the user requests a PDF, ebook, white paper, guide, or another document noun. A supplied sufficient structure stays on the direct path. Use this reference when the unresolved document decision can materially change what the reader can understand, find, compare, decide, or do.

## Working plan

Apply the controller's materiality, delegation, and uncertainty rules. Planning organizes execution; a checkpoint resolves missing material input or decision authority under the request and retained context. Do not make a separate plan document unless it improves review or continuation, or the user requests one. A plan is not the final artifact unless planning itself is the requested job.

Consider these dimensions, exposing only what helps the user understand or revise the approach. Already supplied answers need no reconfirmation.

| Dimension | Useful content |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose and use | Who will use the artifact, in what context, and what must they be able to understand, decide, find, compare, or do? |
| Questions | Which questions must be answered, and which are outside scope? Exploratory questions may evolve after a bounded inspection. |
| Information architecture | Which sections or units are needed, what reader function each serves, and how sequence or modularity supports use. |
| Evidence plan | What is available, what must be retrieved or analyzed, and which gaps could change the answer. |
| Presentation plan | Which comparisons, relationships, actions, or retrieval jobs need prose, tables, charts, diagrams, checklists, worksheets, or interaction, and why. |
| Completion and checkpoints | What makes the result useful and sufficiently supported; any unresolved material choice requiring user input or decision authority, and the work that depends on it. |

Use a short outline with section purposes when that makes direction reviewable. Do not substitute decorative choices such as hero colors, page counts, or card counts for the artifact's information architecture. Resolve ordinary rendering details within the brief and available design capabilities.

## Decision boundaries

Materiality follows the controller: a change in audience, scope, research question, method, positioning, channel, architecture, or commitment matters only when it changes the result or consequential downstream work. These labels do not determine decision authority. Establish whether the request and retained context authorize the agent to choose; do not infer that a topic inherently requires user approval.

- **Sufficient direction:** announce a working plan if useful, then execute. Do not end the turn merely to invite approval.
- **Delegated choice:** investigate enough to choose within scope, retain the choice and its limits, then continue. A recommendation remains a recommendation where adoption was not delegated.
- **Material input or authority still needed:** inspect enough context to recommend a concrete direction and explain the consequence of choosing otherwise. Ask only for the missing decision; wait on dependent work while continuing reusable research or other independent work.

Approval of an outline does not verify its factual assumptions or authorize publication, spending, or other external commitments. Follow the actual authorization for each action. New evidence may require revising a conclusion; preserve unaffected decisions and ask again only if a material choice remains unresolved and the available input or decision authority is insufficient.

## Artifact use contract

Before arranging sections or pages, identify what successful use requires. Do not turn document labels into templates.

Ask only when it can change the architecture:

```text
What must the reader be able to do with this artifact?
```

Relevant use modes can include:

- **linear comprehension** — build an argument, explanation, or recommendation in sequence;
- **skimming** — locate the main answer, implications, or decision-relevant sections quickly;
- **selective lookup** — return later to find one needed unit without reconstructing the whole artifact;
- **comparison** — inspect alternatives or dimensions with low search and memory burden;
- **repeated reference** — reuse stable terminology, procedures, criteria, or evidence over time;
- **work-through** — perform activities in sequence and retain outputs needed by later activities.

These are prompts, not a taxonomy. Several can coexist. Preserve only distinctions that change structure, representation, navigation, or delivery.

Artifact nouns are shorthand rather than routing authority:

```text
EXECUTIVE BRIEF != FIXED LENGTH
GUIDE != FIXED SECTION SEQUENCE
WHITE PAPER != FIXED VISUAL STYLE
WORKBOOK != PROSE + BLANK BOXES
```

If the reader-use contract is already sufficiently supplied, do not reopen it merely because another familiar document type exists.

## Artifact contract

Organize around the reader's questions and use rather than the chronology of research or the internal routing process. A decision brief may lead with a recommendation; an exploratory synthesis may lead with its question and supported findings; a reusable reference may prioritize stable modular retrieval; a working artifact may allocate space and sequence around activities and carried-forward outputs. None requires an invented commercial recommendation.

The finished artifact should let its reader, where material to the use contract:

- Identify the subject, scope, intended use, and period without needing the surrounding chat.
- Find the main answer or findings, then inspect the reasoning and evidence that support them when the artifact is decision- or explanation-oriented. Distinguish observation, interpretation, assumption, and recommendation where confusing them would change meaning.
- Return to a needed unit later without reconstructing unrelated sections when selective lookup or repeated reference is part of the job.
- Trace consequential claims and figures to identifiable sources or supplied data. Preserve relevant population, period, units, method, and uncertainty close enough to prevent misinterpretation; deeper provenance can live in an appendix or source section.
- Understand material counterevidence and limitations at the point where they qualify a conclusion. Do not bury them behind a disclosure or in an unrelated footnote.
- Compare alternatives on relevant common criteria when a choice is requested, and understand the recommendation's trade-off or why the evidence does not yet distinguish them.
- Complete any reader-facing activity with enough instructions, inputs, recording structure, and carry-forward state for the next dependent activity when the artifact is meant to support work rather than only reading.
- Know what follows from the artifact, including a justified no-action result or an evidence limit. Do not force a CTA into an informational artifact.

These are functions, not required headings. Preserve a sufficient supplied outline, and change it only when needed to complete the job truthfully. Visual finish cannot compensate for missing answers, fabricated evidence, unsupported certainty, broken retrieval, or unusable working structure.

## Representation choices

| Form | Use when | Preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prose | Explaining an argument, qualification, mechanism, or recommendation | Connected reasoning; headings that describe the actual content |
| Table | Readers need exact lookup or comparison on common dimensions | Units, comparable definitions, clear headers, and meaningful unknowns rather than invented scores |
| Chart | A supported quantitative pattern is easier to inspect visually | Traceable data, labeled units and scales, comparable periods, and uncertainty where material; never manufacture values for visual completeness |
| Diagram | Structure, dependencies, sequence, or relationships are the subject | Clear meanings for arrows and groups; a proposed flow must not look like an observed causal result |
| Cards or callouts | A few independent findings need separate emphasis | Enough context and qualification; do not fragment a connected argument into decorative tiles |
| Checklist / decision aid | A bounded set of conditions, criteria, or actions benefits from explicit inspection | The decision or action job; do not turn a loose list into a pseudo-procedure |
| Worksheet / exercise | The reader must supply input, make a decision or transformation, or record an output used by the artifact | Required input, instruction, usable recording structure, and the meaning of the resulting output |
| Disclosure or tabs | Secondary detail would otherwise interrupt reading | The main answer and interpretation-changing qualifications remain available; hidden content is discoverable and accessible |
| Interactive controls | Exploring scenarios, filtering, or changing assumptions helps answer the question | Visible input assumptions and scope, labeled modeled outputs, and a useful default state |

Choose the simplest form that improves the reader's task. Keep terminology, units, visual encodings, and hierarchy consistent within the artifact; do not impose an identical layout across different artifacts.

Keep:

```text
VISUAL != DECORATION
CHECKLIST != ACTIONABILITY BY DEFAULT
WORKSHEET != EMPTY SPACE
MORE DESIGNED != MORE USEFUL
```

## Navigation and retrieval

Navigation follows the reader's use, not document length alone.

A short artifact can still need retrieval support when readers will return to separate units. A long artifact may need little explicit navigation when it is intentionally linear and each dependency is clear.

When lookup or repeated reference is material, use the smallest structure that reduces reconstruction burden, such as:

- descriptive and predictable headings;
- stable terminology and labels;
- modular units that preserve enough local context to stand on their own;
- contents, anchors, bookmarks, indexes, or cross-references when they materially improve return use;
- repeated structural cues only when they help readers recognize the same kind of information across units.

Do not optimize retrieval by fragmenting an argument whose meaning depends on connected reasoning. A reusable reference still needs enough context to prevent a locally retrieved statement from becoming misleading.

## Working artifacts

Use this section only when the reader must perform work through the artifact. Do not convert an ordinary guide into exercises merely to make it feel actionable.

For each dependent activity, preserve the smallest meaningful chain:

```text
REQUIRED INPUT
→ ACTIVITY / DECISION / TRANSFORMATION
→ RECORDED OUTPUT
→ CARRIED-FORWARD STATE
→ CHECKPOINT / NEXT DEPENDENCY
```

Ask:

- What must already be known, chosen, or supplied before this activity is usable?
- What exactly is the reader expected to do?
- What result should be recorded or retained?
- Does a later activity depend on that result, and if so is the handoff visible enough to use?
- What condition means the reader can proceed, revisit, or stop?

A workbook can be factually correct and visually polished while still failing if its questions do not produce usable state, if later activities depend on outputs that were never captured, or if the artifact provides blank space without a defined working job.

Do not create a generic workflow object, workbook ontology, scoring system, or mandatory state machine. This is a conditional planning procedure inside the existing artifact owner.

## HTML delivery

Use the host's available implementation or design capabilities when useful. This skill owns content, evidence, use architecture, and decision usefulness; it does not require a framework, component library, hosting service, or another installed skill.

- Use meaningful document structure, descriptive headings in logical order, and navigation when the artifact's use warrants it. Keep reading order coherent independently of visual columns. See [WAI page structure](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/page-structure/).
- Provide text equivalents for information-bearing visuals. A complex chart or diagram needs access to its essential values, relationships, and conclusions, not just a generic image label. See [WAI complex images](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/).
- Let ordinary content reflow on narrow screens and at zoom without loss of information or controls. Where a table or diagram requires two-dimensional layout, contain its scrolling rather than forcing the whole artifact sideways. See [WCAG reflow guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html).
- Make implemented controls operable by keyboard, with meaningful labels and visible focus. Do not rely only on hover or color to expose information. Check applicable contrast and interaction requirements against [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) when implementing; this short contract is not a complete conformance checklist.
- Match delivery to use: a local file, hosted page, printable artifact, or downloadable PDF has different asset and interaction dependencies. Do not deploy simply because HTML was requested. When offline sharing is required, avoid essential remote dependencies; when print/PDF use matters, preserve findings, visual explanations, sources, retrieval cues, and working instructions rather than losing them inside collapsed panels.

Validate the actual artifact with the capabilities available: check claims and data against sources, links and assets, reading order, narrow and wide rendering, implemented interactions, and reader-facing work paths where applicable. Inspect print output when it is part of delivery. State material checks that could not be performed; source inspection or a screenshot alone does not establish accessibility conformance.

If a renderer, scripting, network access, or an asset is unavailable, preserve a useful static narrative and accessible evidence representation where feasible. Do not silently substitute Markdown for requested HTML or imply a preview was tested. Identify the remaining delivery limitation and complete the supported portion.

## Examples

**Resolved HTML report:** "Use this outline and dataset to write an HTML market report." Check data sufficiency, state a brief implementation plan if useful, and build. The format and size do not create a review gate. Ask only if a material ambiguity remains, such as incompatible definitions that change the main comparison.

**Reusable reference:** "Turn these approved procedures into a concise reference our account managers will reopen during calls." Preserve the supplied procedures, organize units for return use, use stable labels and local context, and add only the navigation needed for retrieval. Do not force a report-style main-finding narrative.

**Working guide:** "Turn this expert material into a guide and workbook for accounting-firm owners planning an upmarket move." Preserve supported expertise and message state; structure explanation only where it prepares an activity, define required reader input and recorded output for each real exercise, and carry material outputs into dependent activities. Do not manufacture a maturity score or add worksheets that perform no working job.

**Delegated launch recommendation:** "Research the market and recommend a launch strategy; choose the best approach." Investigate, compare viable directions, recommend one, and complete the requested artifact. Keep unsupported assumptions visible. Do not ask the user to do the strategic selection that was delegated; a recommendation does not authorize launching campaigns.

**User reserves a direction:** "Research both segments, then let me choose before you build the full launch report." Complete the comparison and recommend a segment with the decisive trade-off. Ask for that selection before segment-dependent strategy and production. Continue common market evidence work while waiting; do not treat silence as a selection.

**Small but material:** "Send this short announcement" with two conflicting launch dates and no authoritative resolution. Its length does not eliminate the need to resolve the date before sending. Prepare supported wording while clarifying the date and preserving the actual sending authorization.
