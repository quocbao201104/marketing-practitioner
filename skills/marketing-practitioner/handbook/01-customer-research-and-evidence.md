# 01 — Customer Research and Evidence

## 1. Research begins with a learning purpose

Customer research should answer a bounded learning question or inform a decision. When a choice is already open, identify what the team would decide differently if the answer changed. During exploration, the legitimate purpose may instead be to understand a situation, identify relevant users or barriers, or discover which questions and alternatives deserve further attention. Do not invent a segment choice or intervention merely to make that work look decision-linked.

Public user-research guidance supports research questions that begin broadly and evolve during discovery, as well as turning unsupported stakeholder assumptions into questions [R10][R21].

Bound exploration by the problem or experience of interest, the relevant source scope, and the understanding sought. Inspect enough material to refine the question rather than filtering everything through an untested initial explanation. Stop when the agreed question has a sufficiently grounded answer for its intended use, or state the evidence limit that prevents one. Neither a predetermined commercial answer nor exhaustive customer knowledge is required.

## 2. Qualitative and quantitative evidence answer different questions

Qualitative research is particularly suited to the structure and meaning of experience: situation, trigger, workflow, alternatives, language, perceived risk, objections, switching friction, decision processes, and the mechanisms people believe are operating. It can reveal categories that were not anticipated by the researcher.

Its recurring limitation is inferential scope. Repetition within an interview sample is analytically useful, but it does not by itself estimate population prevalence. The statement "eight of ten interviewees mentioned migration difficulty" describes the sample; it does not establish that eighty percent of the market shares the concern.

Quantitative evidence is better suited to questions of distribution, magnitude, prevalence, frequency, and comparison when measurement and sampling are adequate. Neither qualitative nor ordinary observational quantitative data automatically answer a causal question.

A reported percentage is only as interpretable as the conditions that produced it. When the distinction affects the decision, preserve known sampling frame and coverage, nonresponse, question wording and order, mode, and measurement conditions; the same percentage without those conditions may not support the same inference. This is a quality qualifier, not a survey-methods playbook.

The correct synthesis therefore preserves the difference between:

```text
WHY / HOW / CONTEXT / LANGUAGE
and
HOW MANY / HOW OFTEN / HOW LARGE / WHERE
and
WHAT CHANGES IF WE INTERVENE
```

## 3. Match behavior and stated preference to the question

For questions about what people actually do, prioritize relevant observations and concrete accounts of situations, alternatives, and constraints over unsupported predictions of future behavior. A recalled account is still a report, not direct observation. Stated or hypothetical preferences can inform perceived meaning, desired progress, or possible alternatives; they do not by themselves establish future choice, demand, or willingness to pay in practice.

Behavior is not a universal evidence hierarchy either. Observed choices depend on the options, access, resources, and constraints available at the time; they do not automatically reveal an unconstrained preference or explain why the choice occurred. Evaluate both behavioral and stated material using the question, method, and scope rather than discarding either category by default.

High-value topics include:

- the triggering situation;
- current workflow and workaround;
- attempted alternatives and abandonment reasons;
- desired progress;
- decision criteria;
- perceived risk and switching cost;
- objections and trust requirements;
- who participates in the decision;
- the language used to describe the problem and outcome.

The objective is not to collect colorful quotations. It is to reconstruct the customer's decision environment.

## 4. Preserve four analytical layers

Interview and open-text analysis should preserve a boundary between four layers:

### Source wording

What the participant actually said or what the source actually recorded. Exact wording is valuable because it preserves terminology, framing, emotional texture, and ambiguity.

### Observation

A minimally interpretive statement of what can be grounded in the source.

### Interpretation

A proposed meaning or mechanism inferred from one or more observations.

### Hypothesis

A claim that can guide additional research, measurement, or intervention and is specific enough to be weakened or falsified.

Prematurely merging these layers produces "insight" statements that sound more certain than the underlying evidence permits.

## 5. Voice of Customer is evidence, not copy inventory

Customer language is valuable because it exposes mental models, vocabulary, objections, category expectations, and emotional framing. It should inform copy, but literal quotation is not automatically good messaging. Participants may use language that is idiosyncratic, incomplete, highly contextual, technically inaccurate, or inappropriate for another stage of the buying process.

The analytical task is therefore to preserve original language while identifying which patterns are stable enough to inform message strategy.

## 6. Counterevidence is a required part of synthesis

Research that records only supportive evidence becomes a confirmation system. For any material hypothesis, the synthesis should distinguish:

```text
SUPPORTING
CONTRADICTING
MIXED
UNKNOWN
```

Contradictions can arise because the original proposition is weak, because the market is heterogeneous, because the product or context changed, or because the evidence sources differ in quality. A contradiction is not noise to be removed automatically; it is information about the boundary of the current model.

## 7. Source independence matters

Evidence counts should follow root sources rather than derivative artifacts. One interview may produce several quotations, a summary, a slide, and a strategic memo. These objects do not become independent confirmations simply because they have separate filenames.

For qualitative material, retain root-source identity and the interviewer or prompt context when either changes a decision-relevant interpretation or source independence. Twelve notes derived from two interviews remain two independent sources, even when the notes describe different moments or themes.

When exact wording itself is being used as evidence of spontaneous or customer-origin language, independent participant or root sources do not by themselves establish independent wording origin. Known shared exposure to prior brand copy, interviewer wording, community terminology, or another common upstream language source should constrain claims about independent linguistic convergence without erasing the observed recurrence.

This principle reduces a subtle form of false confidence: repeated processing of the same evidence can create the appearance of convergence without adding information.

## 8. Evidence should carry scope

A finding should be interpretable in relation to at least the dimensions that matter to the decision:

- segment or role;
- customer situation and maturity;
- product or version state;
- geography or market;
- channel;
- acquisition or lifecycle stage;
- time period.

Scope prevents local learning from becoming a universal rule. It also allows apparently contradictory findings to coexist. A switching barrier may be severe for a self-serve small business and negligible for an enterprise whose migration is managed by a dedicated implementation team.

When observed non-action bears on a decision, zero recorded use or response is not rejection unless access, eligibility, opportunity to respond, and instrumentation make non-action interpretable; otherwise it may describe exposure or measurement rather than preference.

## 9. Research synthesis as a bounded output

A high-quality synthesis contains more than themes. It should identify:

1. the exact source set considered;
2. grounded observations;
3. recurring patterns;
4. relevant differences by segment or context;
5. contradictions and exclusions;
6. material unknowns;
7. provisional hypotheses;
8. an answer to the learning question and implications for the decision, when one motivated the research.

It should also state what the research **does not establish**. This final constraint is essential because useful research often reduces uncertainty without eliminating it.

Where it changes the decision, the output should also retain evidence class, root-source independence, and scope alongside the finding. Those qualifiers allow a downstream user to distinguish an exploratory account from a measured pattern without turning every synthesis into a research archive.

## 10. Methodological posture

Customer research should be treated as an iterative model-building process rather than a one-time truth extraction exercise. Findings remain revisable as product capabilities, customer populations, competitive alternatives, and market conditions change.

The appropriate standard is not certainty. It is **traceable inference at the strength justified by the source and method**.
