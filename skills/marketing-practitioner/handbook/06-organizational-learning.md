# 06 — Organizational Learning

## 1. Memory is not file accumulation

Organizational-memory research concerns the acquisition, retention, and retrieval of information within organizations [R09]. For marketing practice, the crucial implication is that a large archive is not automatically useful memory. Information becomes operationally valuable when it changes a later decision.

A team that stores every deck but repeatedly rediscovers the same failed idea has documentation without effective memory.

## 2. Activity history and decision history

Activity history records what happened: a landing page changed, a campaign launched, a price was updated. Decision history records the reasoning context of the change.

A useful decision record contains:

- decision question;
- state of evidence at the time;
- alternatives considered;
- selected option;
- reasons for selection;
- assumptions and uncertainty;
- expected consequences;
- subsequent outcome;
- interpretation of the outcome;
- whether the prior belief changed.

This distinction prevents retrospective storytelling. Without the original decision context, later observers can easily project current knowledge backward and misrepresent why a decision was reasonable or unreasonable at the time.

## 3. Failed hypotheses are assets

Organizations commonly preserve success stories and discard negative learning. This creates repeated search costs and survivorship bias. A failed or weakened hypothesis can be valuable if it records:

```text
HYPOTHESIS
ORIGINAL BASIS
ACTION OR TEST
RESULT
SCOPE
STATUS
REMAINING UNCERTAINTY
```

The status should not be forced into a binary. Useful states include supported, weakened, falsified, inconclusive, insufficient evidence, and superseded by changed conditions.

## 4. Contradiction is a form of knowledge

Conflicting evidence does not necessarily imply analytical failure. It can identify heterogeneity or boundary conditions. A proposition may hold for one segment and fail for another; it may hold before a product change and fail afterward; it may depend on channel or stage of evaluation.

A mature knowledge system therefore allows scoped contradictions to coexist until a broader model explains them.

## 5. Knowledge can become stale

Marketing knowledge is time-dependent. Product capability, pricing, category expectations, competitors, regulation, customer composition, and channels change. A learning record should therefore preserve:

- date or period observed;
- relevant product state;
- segment;
- market;
- channel;
- measurement definition;
- source or design;
- known contradictory evidence;
- freshness or review status when material.

Staleness does not mean old knowledge has no value. Historical knowledge remains useful for understanding change, but it should not silently become current truth.

## 6. Learning claims should be scoped

A useful postmortem states not only what the result supports but also what it does not prove. The minimal structure is:

```text
QUESTION
PRIOR BELIEF
EVIDENCE / DESIGN
RESULT
WHAT THIS SUPPORTS
WHAT THIS WEAKENS
WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE
SCOPE
CONTRADICTIONS
NEXT REUSE
```

The "does not prove" field is especially important because teams often generalize from a narrow result once the original context fades.

## 7. Decision quality improves through retrieval, not storage alone

For retained learning to matter, it must be retrievable at the point of a relevant future decision. The retrieval problem is semantic: the organization must recognize that a new question resembles a prior one even when terminology differs.

Human process can solve this through disciplined reviews and decision logs. Software can assist, but the conceptual requirement remains the same: **memory must enter the decision process**.

## 8. Winner-only memory is systematically biased

If an organization stores only winning experiments, successful campaigns, and accepted strategies, future practitioners receive a distorted model of the search space. They cannot see how many alternatives failed, which risks were discovered, or which apparently attractive ideas repeatedly underperformed.

A useful memory therefore includes:

- rejected options;
- null results;
- negative results;
- invalid or contaminated tests;
- contradictions;
- abandoned assumptions;
- reasons a decision was deferred.

This makes learning less glamorous but more reusable.

## 9. Learning as revision, not accumulation

The objective is not to accumulate more statements about the market. It is to maintain a better current model while preserving history. New evidence can:

- reinforce an existing belief;
- narrow its scope;
- broaden its plausible scope;
- weaken it;
- contradict it;
- supersede it after market or product change.

This model of revision discourages the false idea that a knowledge base should contain a single timeless truth for every marketing question.

## 10. Practical standard

A marketing organization demonstrates learning when it can answer, for a consequential choice:

1. What did we believe before?
2. Why did we believe it?
3. What alternatives did we consider?
4. What did we choose?
5. What happened?
6. What changed in our belief?
7. Where does that learning apply?
8. What should the next team avoid rediscovering?

That capability, rather than archive size, is the practical meaning of organizational marketing memory.
