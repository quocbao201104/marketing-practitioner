# 04 — Messaging, Proof, and Copy

## 1. Messaging as a strategic layer

Messaging determines what the market should understand before copy determines how that understanding is expressed. It translates positioning into a hierarchy of claims, proof, objections, and action. This separation matters because copy can be fluent while the underlying message remains incoherent.

A message strategy should specify:

- the intended reader and situation;
- the category frame;
- the current alternative;
- the desired progress or job;
- the single most important idea to understand;
- the primary value angle;
- reasons to believe;
- objections requiring resolution;
- claims that available evidence permits;
- claims that remain unsupported or prohibited;
- the appropriate next action;
- material uncertainty.

The function of this document is prioritization. A long list of equally weighted benefits is not a message hierarchy.

## 2. Hierarchy before prose

A robust message usually resolves the following conceptual order:

```text
CORE PROMISE
→ WHY IT MATTERS
→ HOW IT WORKS
→ WHY IT IS CREDIBLE
→ OBJECTION RESOLUTION
→ NEXT ACTION
```

The sequence may be rearranged by channel and reader state, but the dependencies remain. A skeptical audience may require proof earlier. A technically sophisticated audience may require mechanism before benefit. A reader already convinced of value may require only risk reduction or implementation detail.

Message design therefore combines strategic content with the **state of the reader at the moment of exposure**.

## 3. Reader-state analysis

Reader-state analysis asks what the recipient is doing, expecting, comparing, doubting, and able to attend to when communication arrives. It is distinct from demographic segmentation. The same person can occupy different states during discovery, comparison, onboarding, failure recovery, renewal, or cancellation.

Reader-state variables include:

- current task;
- prior knowledge;
- attention budget;
- emotional state;
- perceived risk;
- prior exposure to similar promises;
- stage of evaluation;
- immediate decision required.

This perspective is particularly important for microcopy and lifecycle communication because the communication is embedded inside a task rather than consumed as standalone advertising.

## 4. Proof architecture

A claim should be paired with the strongest form of evidence actually available. Possible proof forms include:

- product behavior or mechanism;
- demonstration;
- measured performance with method and scope;
- credible customer testimony;
- independent third-party validation;
- guarantees or contractual commitments;
- transparent limitations that make the promise falsifiable.

Proof should be close enough to the claim that the reader can evaluate the relationship. The strength of the claim and the strength of the evidence should be proportional.

A useful distinction is between **relevance evidence** and **capability evidence**. Customer research can show that a problem matters; it cannot establish that a product performs a capability it does not possess. Product facts constrain what marketing is allowed to say.

## 5. Copy as an expression layer

Copywriting begins after the strategic message is sufficiently resolved. Its job is to preserve meaning while optimizing comprehension, attention, memorability, and action within channel constraints.

The operative principle is reader-first and truth-first:

1. identify the reader's state;
2. identify the simplest accurate explanation;
3. choose the minimum structure required by the format;
4. express concrete value and proof;
5. preserve the intended voice;
6. remove unsupported specificity and promotional filler.

The strongest wording cannot compensate for missing evidence. When material is weak, the correct response is to seek better material or to write a more modest claim.

## 6. Concreteness and processing fluency

Concrete language can improve perceived responsiveness and customer satisfaction in the contexts studied by Packard and Berger [R07]. Processing fluency research also indicates that ease of processing is a meaningful consumer construct [R08]. These findings support clarity, specific nouns, observable mechanisms, and direct verbs as generally useful writing priorities.

They do **not** establish that shorter or more concrete copy always converts better. Complexity can be necessary when the decision is complex. Technical buyers may require precision that increases reading effort but decreases uncertainty. The standard is not minimal word count; it is minimal unnecessary processing burden.

## 7. Format as an information constraint

Different formats create different information budgets and reader expectations.

### Headlines and subject lines

They compete for attention before substantive evaluation. Relevance and clarity normally precede rhetorical novelty. Curiosity is legitimate when the communication identifies its subject and subsequently repays the information gap.

### Landing pages

They can carry category framing, value, mechanism, proof, objection handling, and action. Each section should have a defined information function rather than exist because a template expects it.

### Email

Email combines relationship, timing, and inbox competition. Message design should account for whether the email is expected, transactional, educational, promotional, or recovery-oriented.

### Interface copy

Buttons, errors, confirmations, empty states, and tooltips are part of task execution. Their priority is action clarity, consequence clarity, and error recovery rather than advertising tone.

### Professional social content

Feed-based content competes for attention and social relevance. Useful content generally offers a claim, story, observation, or model that the target professional audience can understand and discuss. Fabricated personal stories or manufactured vulnerability are unacceptable substitutes for source material.

## 8. Human-writing quality

Removing stereotyped AI patterns is not an end in itself. Human-sounding prose can still be strategically weak, inaccurate, vague, or manipulative. Humanization is therefore treated as a quality-control layer.

Common patterns that merit review include:

- significance inflation;
- repetitive promotional adjectives;
- vague appeals to unnamed experts or studies;
- filler clauses that announce importance without adding information;
- forced lists of three;
- unnecessary synonym cycling;
- abstract false ranges;
- excessive nominalization or passive construction where agency matters;
- excessive formatting used to simulate structure;
- generic inspirational conclusions;
- fake candor and manufactured punchlines;
- inflated aphorisms that replace mechanism;
- repetitive sentence rhythm associated with template generation.

These are diagnostic signals, not a prohibition list. A real author's voice may legitimately contain some of them. When a reliable writing sample is supplied, voice calibration should preserve characteristic vocabulary, rhythm, punctuation, formality, and intentional irregularity unless they conflict with truth or task requirements.

## 9. Claim control

Before publication, every material claim should be classified informally into one of four states:

- **supported** — evidence and scope are adequate for the wording;
- **qualified** — a narrower version is supportable;
- **unknown** — evidence is insufficient;
- **forbidden** — the claim conflicts with known facts, policy, law, ethics, or explicit product limitations.

This discipline is particularly important for comparative claims, quantified outcomes, guarantees, security/privacy statements, regulated topics, and claims about market leadership.

## 10. Copy quality is multidimensional

A copy review should not begin with whether the prose is "good." It should evaluate, in order:

1. target and situation fit;
2. positioning coherence;
3. message priority;
4. factual support;
5. proof adequacy;
6. objection coverage;
7. clarity and concreteness;
8. channel fit;
9. voice and naturalness;
10. CTA coherence.

Stylistic polish is downstream of strategic correctness.
